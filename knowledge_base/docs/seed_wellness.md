## Q: How much water should I drink daily?
A: Most adults need about 8–10 cups (2–2.5 litres) of water per day. Your needs increase with exercise, hot weather, or if you're breastfeeding. A simple guide: your urine should be pale yellow — darker means drink more.

## Q: Does coffee count toward my daily hydration?
A: Yes. Despite the mild diuretic effect of caffeine, research shows caffeinated drinks still contribute net fluid to your intake. That said, plain water is still the best hydrator.

## Q: What are signs of dehydration?
A: Common signs include dark yellow urine, dry mouth, fatigue, headache, and dizziness. Severe dehydration can cause rapid heartbeat and confusion — if that happens, seek medical attention.

## Q: How many hours of sleep do adults need?
A: Most adults need 7–9 hours per night. Consistently getting fewer than 7 hours is linked to impaired concentration, weakened immunity, and higher risk of chronic disease.

## Q: What are good sleep hygiene habits?
A: Keep a consistent sleep and wake time (even on weekends), avoid screens 30–60 minutes before bed, keep your bedroom cool and dark, and limit caffeine after 2 pm. A short wind-down routine like reading or stretching signals your brain it's time to sleep.

## Q: Why do I feel groggy after 9+ hours of sleep?
A: Oversleeping can cause sleep inertia — a groggy, foggy feeling. It may also signal an underlying issue like sleep apnea or depression. If it happens often, talk to a healthcare provider.

## Q: What should I eat for sustained energy throughout the day?
A: Prioritise complex carbohydrates (oats, brown rice, sweet potato), lean protein (chicken, eggs, legumes), and healthy fats (avocado, nuts). Avoid ultra-processed foods and sugary snacks that cause energy spikes followed by crashes.

## Q: How much protein do I need per day?
A: A general guideline is 0.8 g per kg of body weight for sedentary adults. If you exercise regularly or are building muscle, aim for 1.2–2.0 g/kg. Spread intake across meals for best absorption.

## Q: Are carbohydrates bad for you?
A: No. Carbohydrates are your brain and muscles' preferred fuel source. The quality matters — choose whole grains, legumes, fruits, and vegetables over refined sugars and white flour.

## Q: How can I reduce stress naturally?
A: Proven techniques include regular aerobic exercise (even a 20-minute walk), diaphragmatic breathing (slow 4-count inhale, 6-count exhale), mindfulness meditation, spending time in nature, and maintaining social connections.

## Q: What is box breathing and how do I do it?
A: Box breathing is a calming technique used by athletes and the military. Inhale for 4 counts, hold for 4, exhale for 4, hold for 4. Repeat 4–6 cycles. It activates the parasympathetic nervous system and reduces acute stress within minutes.

## Q: How does exercise affect mental health?
A: Exercise releases endorphins and increases serotonin and dopamine — neurotransmitters that improve mood. Even 20–30 minutes of moderate cardio 3–5 times per week is associated with reduced anxiety and depression symptoms.

## Q: How many steps should I aim for per day?
A: 7,000–10,000 steps per day is associated with significant health benefits. If you're sedentary, start with a 10-minute walk and build gradually — any increase from your baseline is beneficial.

## Q: What is HIIT and is it better than steady-state cardio?
A: HIIT (High-Intensity Interval Training) alternates short bursts of intense effort with recovery periods. It burns more calories per minute and improves cardiovascular fitness faster, but steady-state cardio is easier to sustain and better for recovery days. Both are valuable.

## Q: How long should a beginner workout be?
A: 20–30 minutes is plenty when starting out. Consistency matters more than duration. Three 20-minute sessions per week beats one 90-minute session you burn out from.

## Q: What vitamins are important for energy?
A: B vitamins (B1, B2, B3, B6, B12) are essential for converting food into energy. Iron deficiency is a common cause of fatigue, especially in women. Vitamin D and magnesium also play roles in energy and mood. A balanced diet covers most needs; supplements are warranted only if deficient.

## Q: Is intermittent fasting healthy?
A: Intermittent fasting (e.g., 16:8 — eating within an 8-hour window) can support weight management and metabolic health for many people. It is not suitable for everyone, including pregnant women, people with a history of eating disorders, or those on certain medications. Consult a healthcare provider before starting.

## Q: How can I improve my mood through diet?
A: Foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids (fatty fish, flaxseed, walnuts), magnesium (dark leafy greens, nuts), and fermented foods (yoghurt, kefir) support brain health and mood. Limit alcohol and ultra-processed foods, which are associated with higher rates of depression.

## Q: What is the difference between soluble and insoluble fibre?
A: Soluble fibre (oats, beans, apples) dissolves in water and helps lower cholesterol and stabilise blood sugar. Insoluble fibre (whole wheat, vegetables) adds bulk to stool and supports gut health. Aim for 25–38 g total fibre per day from a variety of sources.

## Q: How can I build a sustainable exercise habit?
A: Start smaller than you think you need to. Attach exercise to an existing habit (e.g., walk after lunch). Track your streak. Choose activities you actually enjoy — adherence beats optimisation. Rest days are not failures; they are part of the programme.
